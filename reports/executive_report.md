# Customer Intelligence Review — Executive Report

**To:** Managing Director; Commercial Director; Finance Director; Head of CRM
**Data:** all recorded transactions, 1 December 2009 – 9 December 2011 (1.07M lines)
**Status:** analysis complete; recommendations for decision

---

## 1. The bottom line

Revenue looks stable, but the customer base underneath it is weakening. Merchandise revenue grew
**1.7%** year on year, yet sales to **identifiable customers fell 2.5%**. All of the growth came
from guest buyers we cannot track, whose sales rose **32%**. Only **64%** of last year's customers
bought again, and those who stayed spent less: **net revenue retention was 81%**.

We estimate **£0.89M of the next twelve months' revenue is at risk** (11% of the current run-rate).
That figure excludes further spend decline among customers who stay, so the true retention problem is larger.
Most of that risk is spread across many small and mid-sized accounts. A focused group of
**107 high-value accounts (£0.47M of annual revenue)** is showing warning signs now.

**We recommend three decisions:**
1. Launch a key-account rescue programme this month, starting with the 107 accounts.
2. Replace uniform marketing effort with automated, segment-specific retention programmes.
3. Make customer identification at checkout a board-level KPI.

All three programmes should be run with control groups so their return can be measured rather than assumed.

## 2. What we found — and what management should do differently

### Finding 1 — Growth is coming from customers we cannot see
| FY = Dec–Nov | FY2010 | FY2011 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net merchandise revenue | £9.15M | £9.30M | **+1.7%** |
| Sales to identified customers | £8.18M | £7.97M | **−2.5%** |
| Guest sales (no customer ID) | £1.07M | £1.41M | **+32.1%** |
| Active identified customers | 4,227 | 4,284 | +1.3% |
| Orders per customer | 4.27 | 4.09 | −4.4% |
| Average order value (identified) | £452.67 | £455.17 | +0.6% |

About 15% of sales cannot be linked to any customer. For those sales there is no retention, no targeting and no lifetime value.
New identified customers in the Sep–Nov peak fell from 932 (2010) to 596 (2011). The data cannot
tell whether acquisition genuinely weakened or new buyers are simply checking out as guests.

**Do differently:** make customer identification a tracked KPI. Require an account or email at
checkout, or offer an incentive to create one. Until this is fixed, every customer metric
describes only ~85% of the business.
*Owner: E-commerce. Timing: next quarter.*

### Finding 2 — Retention, not acquisition, is the bigger lever
- Of 4,227 customers active in FY2010, **2,699 (64%)** bought again in FY2011.
- The **1,528 who did not** had spent **£1.02M** the year before.
- Customers who stayed spent less overall. Revenue from the FY2010 base fell from £8.09M to £6.53M (NRR 81%).
- New customers are most likely to leave straight after their first order: **only 21% order again in their first month**.

**Do differently:** set a retention target (segment-level NRR) alongside the sales target. Fund a
**second-order programme** for new customers within 60–90 days of their first order. Customers
with a single purchase day in a year churn at **61%**, versus **11%** for those with 5–9.
*Owner: CRM. Timing: next quarter.*

### Finding 3 — Revenue is highly concentrated
- The top **10 customers** generate **16%** of revenue.
- The top **1%** generate **31%**.
- The top **20%** generate **77%**.

Losing even a handful of accounts would be material. It also means key-account management is worth
investing in where it is targeted.

**Do differently:** give the top tier (Protect segment, 710 accounts, £5.24M annual revenue) a named
account owner and a quarterly review. Do not, however, spend account-manager time uniformly. Under
illustrative cost assumptions, calling every low-risk key account does not pay back; calling
the at-risk ones does (Section 3).
*Owner: Commercial. Timing: ongoing.*

### Finding 4 — Revenue at risk is £0.89M, and it is spread across many accounts
Each active customer's trailing-year revenue was multiplied by their modelled probability of
placing no order in the next 12 months.

| Management segment | Customers | Revenue, last 12m | Revenue at risk | Action |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1. Protect – Key Accounts | 710 | £5.24M | £175k | Named owner, quarterly review |
| **2. Rescue – High Value at Risk** | **107** | **£0.47M** | **£92k** | **Account-manager call within 2 weeks** |
| 3. Develop – Core Growth | 1,020 | £1.19M | £217k | Cadence-timed replenishment & cross-sell |
| 4. Win Back – Core at Risk | 108 | £0.11M | £54k | Triggered win-back sequence |
| 5. Nurture – New Customers | 591 | £0.38M | £113k | Second-order programme |
| 6. Automate – Low Value Active | 834 | £0.32M | £107k | Low-cost self-service |
| 7. Deprioritise – Low Value Lapsing | 880 | £0.21M | £130k | One low-cost reactivation; survey |
| 8. Lapsed (>12m) – Win-back pool | 1,589 | – | – | Pre-season campaign (historic spend £1.18M) |
| **Total (active)** | **4,250** | **£7.94M** | **£887k** | |

Two points matter for how this is resourced:
- **The risk is diffuse.** It takes 978 accounts to cover half of the revenue at risk, so account
  managers cannot address it alone. Automated programmes for the Develop, Nurture and Win Back segments
  cover most of the £ at stake.
- **High-value customers rarely look risky in probability terms** (median 6%), but losing one is
  costly. The Rescue trigger is therefore more sensitive: it flags a key account at a 25% churn
  probability, or when it is 30+ days overdue against its *own* ordering rhythm. Example: a
  £56k-a-year account that normally orders weekly and has been silent for 39 days.

**Do differently:** call the Rescue list this month, largest accounts first, and record the reason
for each slowdown (price, range, service, competitor). That reason data does not exist today and
is the most valuable thing the programme will produce.
*Owner: Commercial. Timing: this month.*

### Finding 5 — The business is seasonal, and retention should be planned around the season
- Sales peak **September–November**.
- Customer activity in November is roughly double January's (25% vs 12% monthly active rate for existing cohorts).
- Customers acquired in the peak look weak in the following quiet months, but they return for the next peak at similar rates.

**Do differently:** run reactivation and win-back campaigns **before** September, not after Christmas.
Compare acquisition cohorts season-to-season, not month-to-month.
*Owner: CRM. Timing: annual calendar.*

## 3. Is it worth doing? (Scenario — assumptions clearly marked)

The data contains **no margin, cost or campaign-response data**, so the business case below uses
**illustrative assumptions**. Each can be changed in `excel/retention_investment_model.xlsx`.

| Assumption (to be replaced by Finance / CRM) | Value used |
|---|---|
| Gross margin | 40% |
| Save rate, Rescue segment | 25% of revenue at risk |
| Cost per account-manager contact / automated contact | £40 / £0.50–£2 |

On these assumptions:
- **The Rescue programme breaks even at an 11.7% save rate.**
- Automated programmes for the Develop, Win Back and Nurture segments return several times their cost.
- Blanket account-manager coverage of low-risk key accounts does not pay back.

The size of the prize is modest in retention-of-churners terms: about **£90k recovered revenue** at
the illustrative save rates, set against £7.9M of trailing revenue at stake. Most of the value lies in:
- **stopping shrinkage** among retained customers (NRR 81%);
- **improving second-order conversion**;
- **capturing the 15% of sales from unidentified buyers.**

These should be the programme's success measures, not just "churners saved".

**Save rates must be measured, not assumed.** Every programme should hold out a random 10–20% of
eligible customers so the incremental effect can be proven before scaling.

### Appendix — method in one paragraph
Two Excel sheets were combined and de-duplicated (their 1–9 Dec 2010 overlap removed), and every
line was classified (sale, return, write-off, stock adjustment, non-merchandise). Orders that were
keyed and immediately cancelled were matched and excluded from order counts. A star-schema
warehouse (DuckDB/SQL) produces all KPIs. Customer value was modelled with BG/NBD and Gamma-Gamma
models, validated out-of-sample against a naive benchmark. Churn risk was modelled with a
logistic regression on behaviour in the trailing 12 months, trained on a seasonally matched cutoff
and checked for calibration. Customers were then placed in eight management segments by current
value and forward risk. Full detail: `docs/` and `notebooks/`.

Figures: `reports/figures/01–12`.
